外企行为面试核心：STAR法则（Situation-Task-Action-Result），描述经历时统一套用，回答简洁，突出成果。
一、开场与背景 / Opening & Background
1. Tell me about yourself. / 请介绍一下你自己。

Sure. My name is Qingqing Lei, and I’m a full-stack engineer with about seven years of experience. I started as a Python developer, then moved into frontend, so I’m comfortable on both sides. Right now I work at Nokia Shanghai Bell. I build enterprise systems with React and TypeScript, and I also write Python APIs. Recently I’ve been working on an LLM agent for a telecom platform — basically helping people do complex configuration in plain language. I like taking a messy business problem and turning it into something people can actually use. That’s me in a nutshell.

2. Walk me through your resume. / 请带我过一遍你的简历。

Sure. I studied Network Engineering at Huaibei Normal University, so I already had some CS and networking background. After graduation I joined Jinyi as a Python developer, working on coal procurement, sales, and quality inspection. Then I switched to frontend and spent about five years there — React, TypeScript, mini programs, and some BI reports. Last year I joined Nokia. I maintain a legacy AngularJS system, build the new React version, and I also built an AI assistant with LangChain and RAG. So the path is pretty clear: backend first, then frontend, and now more full-stack plus AI.

3. Tell me about your most recent role. / 请介绍一下你最近的一份工作。

I’m a frontend engineer at Nokia Shanghai Bell, but the work is pretty full-stack. I own core features in a telecom equipment management system — device status, topology, configuration, alarms. There’s an old AngularJS app and a new React one, so I work on both. I also pushed coding standards, upgraded the UI library, and improved WebSocket stability for cutover tasks. On the side, I built an intelligent assistant with LangGraph and RAG, so operators can do configuration in natural language. It’s a mix of product delivery, technical cleanup, and AI.

4. What made you choose your major? / 你为什么选择这个专业？

Honestly, I chose Network Engineering because I was curious how the internet actually works — not just using apps, but how data moves from A to B. At university I liked the practical side more than pure theory: protocols, systems, troubleshooting. That kind of thinking still helps me now. When a page is slow or a WebSocket drops, I don’t only look at the UI. I think about the whole path — client, API, network, backend. So the major wasn’t random. It trained me to see systems, not just screens.

5. What made you interested in this industry? / 你为什么对这个行业感兴趣？

I’ve always liked building tools that people use every day at work. At Jinyi it was procurement and bidding. At Nokia it’s telecom operations. Those are messy, real workflows, not demo pages. Now I’m interested in auto manufacturing for a similar reason. A car factory is also a big system — production, quality, data, people. Volkswagen Anhui is doing EVs and a lot of digital work in Hefei. I think my experience with enterprise systems and AI agents can actually help on the factory and software side, not just look cool on a resume.

二、求职动机与岗位匹配 / Motivation & Fit
6. Why are you interested in this role? / 你为什么对这个职位感兴趣？

Because it’s full-stack work with a real business behind it, not just making pages look nice. From what I understand, this role is about building internal platforms, connecting systems, and maybe bringing AI into production. That’s very close to what I already do. At Nokia I work across React, Python, APIs, and an LLM agent. I like being close to the actual operation, talking to users, and shipping something they can use the next day. This role feels like that, but in the EV manufacturing world, which I really want to grow into.

7. Why do you want to work for our company? / 你为什么想加入我们公司？

Two honest reasons. First, I’m from Anhui — I studied in Huaibei — so Hefei is not a random city for me. I actually want to build a long-term career here. Second, Volkswagen Anhui is not a normal assembly plant. It’s Volkswagen’s first majority-controlled company in mainland China, and Hefei is a real EV hub for the group. You make CUPRA Tavascan for Europe and ID.UNYX for China. For a full-stack engineer, that means software, data, and production are all in one place. I want to be part of that, not just watch it from Shanghai.

8. Why should we hire you? / 我们为什么要录用你？

Because I can deliver on both sides of the stack, and I don’t need a long ramp-up to start helping. I’ve built Python services, React apps, mini programs, and recently an AI agent with RAG and tool calling. I also have a lot of experience with messy legacy systems, which factories always have. I’m used to talking with business people, QA, and backend, and I care about finishing the job, not just writing code. You don’t just get a frontend person or a backend person. You get someone who can connect the pieces.

9. What can you bring to this team? / 你能为这个团队带来什么？

Three things. First, delivery. I’m used to taking a requirement from a document all the way to production. Second, full-stack range. I can do React, TypeScript, Python, SQL, and I can glue systems together with APIs. Third, I’ve already worked with AI in a serious way — agents, RAG, evaluation, human-in-the-loop — not just chatting with ChatGPT. I think that mix is useful here, especially if the team is building internal tools or AI for production. I also communicate pretty directly, so I won’t hide problems until the last minute.

10. What are you looking for in your next role? / 你在下一份工作中想寻找什么？

I want a place where the product is real, and I can stay long enough to go deep. In my next role I want to do full-stack work closer to the business — factory, operations, internal platforms — and keep growing on the AI side. I also want a team that speaks English at work, because that’s how I want to keep improving. Volkswagen Anhui fits that. It’s in my home province, the industry is going toward software-defined vehicles, and the work looks more meaningful than another e-commerce admin page.

三、自我认知与职业规划 / Self-Awareness & Career Goals
11. What are your strengths? / 你的优势是什么？

I’d say two main strengths. One is I pick things up fast and still care about quality. I went from Python to React, then to AngularJS, then to LangGraph, and I still delivered. The other is I think about the whole problem, not only my ticket. If a form is buggy, I look at the data, the API, and the user flow. My manager at Nokia said I don’t just follow instructions — I actually think the project through. That’s probably the most useful thing I bring to a team.

12. What is your greatest weakness? / 你最大的缺点是什么？

I can be a bit too hands-on. If something is unclear, my first instinct is to just stay late and figure it out myself. That sounds responsible, but sometimes it slows the team down, because I should have asked earlier. I’ve been working on this. Now I set a time box — if I’m still stuck after a short period, I write down what I already tried and ask. It feels a bit uncomfortable, but the results are better. I get unblocked faster, and other people can actually help me.

13. Where do you see yourself in five years? / 你五年后想达到什么位置？

In five years I want to be a strong full-stack engineer in the auto digital space, someone who can own a platform end to end. Not necessarily a people manager on day one, but someone the team trusts with architecture, delivery, and a bit of AI. I’d like to understand manufacturing better — MES, production data, how software actually helps the line. If I do this job well, I hope I can grow with Volkswagen Anhui, maybe later take more ownership of internal tools or an AI platform. That’s the direction, not a fancy title.

14. What are your short-term and long-term career goals? / 你的短期和长期职业目标是什么？

Short term, I want to join a team like this and become useful quickly — learn the business, ship features, and not need too much babysitting. In the first year I also want to get more solid on backend and deployment, things like Docker and system integration. Long term, I want to be a full-stack person who can design tools for manufacturing and operations, including AI agents that are actually safe to use. I don’t want to jump companies every year. I want one place where the domain gets deeper every year.

15. How would your colleagues describe you? / 你的同事会怎么形容你？

I think they’d say I’m reliable and pretty easy to work with. If I say I’ll finish something, I finish it. They’d also say I ask a lot of questions about the business, not only the UI. At Jinyi, people used to come to me when a form or a data issue was messy, because I’d sit down and trace it. At Nokia, I think they’d say I pick up old systems fast. And maybe that I stay a bit late when something is interesting. That’s fair. I do that.

四、行为面试：团队协作 / Behavioral: Teamwork
16. Tell me about a time you worked in a team. / 请讲一次你团队合作的经历。

On the online bidding platform, we had B-end, C-end, and shared components in one monorepo. My task was the rich-text editor and some global state, but it only worked if the shared package was clean. So I sat with the other frontend people, aligned the APIs, and we used pnpm to keep the common parts in one place. When something broke, we didn’t blame one app. We looked at the shared layer first. In the end, both sides shipped faster, and we didn’t copy-paste the same component three times. That’s the kind of teamwork I like — clear ownership, shared code.

17. Tell me about a time you worked with a diverse team. / 请讲一次你和多元化团队合作的经历。

Nokia is pretty international, so even in China you work with different styles and sometimes English docs, English tickets, English reviews. On the telecom system, I had to align with backend, QA, and people who knew the old AngularJS world very well. Some people wanted to move fast on React, some were very careful because production devices are sensitive. I learned to slow down my explanation, write things down, and confirm in the meeting, not only in chat. It took a bit more time, but we avoided a lot of “I thought you meant something else.” That’s diversity in a practical way.

18. Describe a time you had to collaborate with another department. / 请描述一次你与其他部门协作的经历。

At Jinyi, the procurement system touched warehouse, finance, and lab testing. One time a settlement page looked fine in UI, but finance said the numbers were wrong. I didn’t argue about the frontend. I sat with them, compared their Excel with our SQL, and found the rule was different for some coal types. Then I worked with backend to fix the calculation and added clearer validation on the form. After that, the complaints dropped. The lesson was simple: when another department is unhappy, go to their workflow first, not to your component.

19. Tell me about a time you supported a teammate. / 请讲一次你支持队友的经历。

When we started the React version of the telecom system, one teammate was strong in business but new to the new stack. He was stuck on the topology page. I didn’t just take over. I sat with him, we drew the data flow, and I showed how I would split the XFlow part from the business state. Then he implemented it, and I only reviewed. Later he could do similar pages himself. It was slower that day, but the team got another person who could deliver. I think that’s real support — not hero coding for someone.

20. How do you handle a team member who is not pulling their weight? / 如果团队中有人不出力，你会怎么处理？

I wouldn’t start in a group meeting. That’s just embarrassing. I’d talk to the person privately first: “I noticed this part is slipping, is something blocked?” Sometimes it’s unclear requirements, not laziness. If they need help, I help them break the work down. If it keeps happening, then I bring facts to the lead — what’s delayed, what’s the impact — not a personality complaint. The goal is to protect the delivery, not to win an argument. I’ve seen this work better than calling someone out in public.

21. Tell me about a time you had to build consensus. / 请讲一次你促成共识的经历。

When I wanted to upgrade the UI component library at Nokia, some people said, “Don’t touch it, production is stable.” Others wanted the new version for accessibility and maintenance. I didn’t push by opinion. I made a small spike: upgrade in one low-risk module, list the breaking changes, and show a rollback plan. Then we agreed to do it in slices, not a big bang. People accepted it because the risk was visible. Consensus, for me, is not everyone being happy. It’s everyone seeing the same risk and the same plan.

22. Describe a time you worked with someone whose working style was very different from yours. / 请描述一次你和风格迥异的人合作的经历。

I like to write things down and ship in small pieces. I once worked with a backend engineer who liked to keep everything in his head and change a lot at once. At first we kept missing each other — my UI was ready, his API had already changed. I asked if we could freeze a simple contract first, even a markdown table of fields. He said okay, as long as I didn’t over-document. After that, we were fine. I didn’t try to make him like my style. We just found one shared habit that protected both of us.

23. Tell me about a time you had to rely on others to get something done. / 请讲一次你依靠他人完成任务的经历。

The AI assistant looks like a frontend product, but it only works if platform tools, NETCONF, and knowledge docs are right. I could not do the device push myself. So I had to rely on the platform team for the real operations, and on domain people for which answers were allowed. I wrapped their operations as tools, and we used human-in-the-loop when a parameter was missing. My job was to make the collaboration clear: what I need, when I need it, what happens if it fails. Without them, the agent is just a chatbot.

24. How do you build trust with new team members? / 你如何与新团队成员建立信任？

Pretty simple. I try to be predictable. I reply, I share context, and I deliver the first small thing on time. I also don’t hide what I don’t know. If I’m new to a module, I’ll say, “I haven’t touched this yet, I’ll check and come back this afternoon.” People trust that more than fake confidence. And I give credit in public when they help me. Trust is not a speech. It’s a few weeks of not surprising people in a bad way.

25. Tell me about a time you gave credit to someone else. / 请讲一次你把功劳归给他人的经历。

When the agent assistant started giving better answers, people said, “Nice work on the AI.” But a lot of the quality came from a teammate who helped me clean the knowledge base — the chunking, the metadata, the source tracing. In the review I said that clearly: the model is not magic, the documents are. I also put his name in the notes. It wasn’t fake humility. If I don’t give that credit, next time nobody wants to do the unsexy data work. And that data work is actually the product.

五、行为面试：领导力与主动性 / Behavioral: Leadership & Initiative
26. Tell me about a time you showed leadership. / 请讲一次你展现领导力的经历。

I’m not a manager, but I led the UI library upgrade. The situation was: the old version was blocking us, but nobody wanted to own the risk. I wrote a short plan, split the work by module, and checked in with people every few days. When something broke, I didn’t say “your page.” I helped fix it, then updated the checklist so the next module was easier. We finished without stopping the business features. For me, leadership is just making the path clear and taking the first unpleasant part yourself.

27. Tell me about a time you took initiative. / 请讲一次你主动采取行动的经历。

The intelligent assistant wasn’t a fully ready assignment on day one. I saw operators struggling with complex configuration, so I spent my own time researching LangChain and LangGraph, then built it step by step — intent routing, RAG, tool calling, human confirmation. I showed a demo, got feedback, then made it more strict: cite sources, refuse when evidence is weak. People started using it because it reduced the operational complexity. I didn’t wait for a perfect PRD. I made something small enough to argue with.

28. Describe a time you led a project from start to finish. / 请描述一次你从头到尾负责一个项目的经历。

At Jinyi I independently built the chemical-product and raw-coal procurement modules — from purchase, to lab testing, to settlement. I started by mapping the workflow with the business, then I designed the Django models, built the pages, and sat with QA until it shipped. After go-live I still owned the production issues. It wasn’t glamorous, but it was end to end. I learned that “done” is not merge. Done is when warehouse and finance can actually use it for a week without calling you every hour.

29. Tell me about a time you motivated others. / 请讲一次你激励他人的经历。

When we introduced ESLint and Prettier on the React project, people were annoyed at first. Too many red lines. I didn’t say “just follow the rule.” I fixed the worst files myself, added the config so format-on-save was easy, and showed that code review became shorter. After a week, the complaints dropped because the pain was front-loaded. Motivation, in engineering, is often just removing friction and showing a quick win. People don’t need a speech. They need the tool to stop fighting them.

30. Tell me about a time you had to lead without formal authority. / 请讲一次你没有正式职权却发挥领导作用的经历。

The coding-standard rollout was exactly that. I couldn’t force anyone. I had to make the standard easy and make the benefit visible. I set up ESLint and Prettier, explained it in a short note, and offered to help on the first PR. Some people ignored it until their review comments got shorter. Then they joined. No title, just usefulness. If you want people to follow you without authority, you have to reduce their work, not add a new process on top.

31. Describe a time you mentored or coached someone. / 请描述一次你指导或辅导他人的经历。

A newer teammate had to work on the AngularJS project and was a bit scared of it. I told him, honestly, I was also new to it not long ago. I walked him through one real ticket: where the route is, where the service is, how the Material component is used. Then I let him do the next ticket and only reviewed. I also wrote a tiny cheat sheet, not a huge wiki nobody reads. After two weeks he could ship small features himself. Coaching for me is one real task, not a lecture.

32. Tell me about a time you delegated work effectively. / 请讲一次你有效分配工作的经历。

On the bidding platform monorepo, I couldn’t do B-end, C-end, and the shared rich-text at the same time. I kept the editor and the data migration script, because I already understood Lexical. I asked another person to own the listing pages, and we agreed on the shared component API first. I didn’t dump leftover work. I delegated a whole slice, with a clear interface. That way I wasn’t a bottleneck, and they didn’t need to ping me for every button. Delegation only works if the boundary is clear.

33. Tell me about a time you had to make a difficult decision. / 请讲一次你做出艰难决定的经历。

On the telecom system, we had a choice: keep patching AngularJS forever, or invest in the React version while still supporting the old one. Rewriting everything at once was not realistic. I pushed for a boring decision: new features that are big go to React when we can, but we still upgrade AngularJS Material so the old system doesn’t rot. It was slower than a “let’s rewrite” story. But production stayed up, and we reduced technical debt. The hard part was saying no to a beautiful rewrite.

34. Describe a time you challenged the status quo. / 请描述一次你挑战现状的经历。

On the bidding platform, the old build was slow, and people just accepted it. I suggested moving to Vite and putting B-end, C-end, and shared code into a pnpm monorepo. Some people said, “If it works, don’t touch it.” I did a trial on one app first, showed the build time, then we rolled it out. Later we also used a Node script to migrate rich-text JSON to HTML, instead of doing it by hand. Challenging the status quo, for me, is a small proof, not a big complaint in a meeting.

35. Tell me about a time you turned around a failing project. / 请讲一次你扭转失败项目的经历。

The cutover-task progress on the telecom system was unreliable because the WebSocket path was unstable. Users didn’t trust the page, so they kept calling people. I treated it as a failing feature, not a small bug. I traced the connection path, added better reconnect and status, and made the UI show real progress instead of a spinner. After that, people could watch the task without guessing. It wasn’t a whole project reboot. It was: find the thing users already don’t believe, and make it honest again.

六、行为面试：冲突、沟通与影响力 / Behavioral: Conflict, Communication & Influence
36. Tell me about a time you had a conflict with a coworker. / 请讲一次你和同事发生冲突的经历。

I had a conflict with a backend colleague about an API. I wanted a clearer contract for a complex form. He felt I was slowing him down with “frontend opinions.” We were both a bit sharp in the group chat. I asked to talk offline. It turned out he was under a deadline, and I was scared of another last-minute field change. We agreed: freeze the fields for this sprint, and I’ll handle display mapping on my side. After that we were fine. The conflict wasn’t personal. It was two fears meeting each other.

37. Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager. / 请讲一次你与经理意见不合的经历。

Once my manager wanted a feature squeezed into a release that was already tight. I said I could do a thin version, but not the full flow with testing. I didn’t just say no. I showed what would slip — especially the edge cases in the form. We agreed to ship the main path and put the extra cases in the next sprint. He was okay because I offered a plan, not a complaint. Disagreeing with a manager is fine. Surprising them on release day is not.

38. How do you handle conflict in a team? / 你如何处理团队中的冲突？

I try to move it from opinions to facts. What is the user impact, what is the deadline, what is the risk? I also prefer a short call or a room, not a long comment thread. If two people are stuck, I’ll restate both sides so they feel heard, then suggest a trial: do A in a small scope, keep B as fallback. Conflict is normal when people care. It becomes toxic only when we talk about who is right, not what we should ship.

39. Tell me about a time you had to persuade someone. / 请讲一次你说服他人的经历。

I had to persuade the team to treat RAG answers more strictly. Some people wanted the agent to always reply, even if the knowledge base was weak. I showed a few wrong answers and how dangerous that is in a telecom setting. Then I proposed: cite the source, and refuse if evidence is not enough. We also stored evaluation results. They agreed after they saw a bad answer, not after my speech. Persuasion in engineering is usually a demo of the failure, plus a simple rule people can follow.

40. Describe a time you had to influence without authority. / 请描述一次你没有职权却成功影响他人的经历。

Same story as the linting and the UI upgrade, honestly. I couldn’t assign people. I could only make the first version, show the benefit, and help with the painful migration. For the agent, I also had to get domain people to review answers. I didn’t say “please do extra work.” I said, “If you review these ten questions, we can stop the bot from saying something wrong to operators.” They helped because the risk was theirs too. No authority, just a shared problem.

41. Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news. / 请讲一次你传递坏消息的经历。

Before a bidding-platform release, I found the rich-text migration would not be fully clean by the original date. I told my lead the same day, not the night before. I explained what was ready, what was risky, and two options: delay a little, or ship with a fallback to the old format. We chose a small delay and a migration script. The bad news was still bad, but it was usable. I learned: bad news with a next step is communication. Bad news with only panic is just noise.

42. Describe a time you had to deal with a difficult stakeholder or client. / 请描述一次你应对难缠利益相关者或客户的经历。

On the procurement system, one business user kept changing the contract form after we had already built it. He was not trying to be difficult — his process was just messy. I started recording each change as: must-have for this month, or nice-to-have later. I also sat with him and clicked through the form, so he could see the cost of one more field. After that, the requests got more focused. With difficult stakeholders, I don’t argue personality. I make the trade-off visible on the screen.

43. Tell me about a time you had to say no to someone. / 请讲一次你拒绝他人的经历。

A business person asked me to add a lot of extra display fields right before a settlement release. I said no to doing it in that sprint. I explained that if I add them now, testing the calculation would be rushed, and finance already had issues before. I offered a date in the next iteration. He wasn’t happy that day, but he accepted it because I said what I would do, not just what I wouldn’t. Saying no is part of protecting the thing they actually need.

44. How do you handle feedback from peers? / 你如何处理来自同事的反馈？

I try not to defend in the first ten seconds. If it’s a code review, I read the comment, check it, and either change it or explain the constraint. If they’re right, I just say thanks and fix it. If I disagree, I ask, “What problem are you seeing?” Sometimes they’re protecting a case I missed. I like peer feedback, because it catches my blind spots earlier than production. The only feedback I struggle with is vague stuff like “this doesn’t feel good,” so I’ll ask for one example.

45. Tell me about a time you had to present to senior leadership. / 请讲一次你向高层汇报的经历。

I presented the intelligent assistant to senior people, not as a model demo, but as a workflow. I showed: user asks in natural language, we check knowledge, we confirm risky steps, then we call tools. I also showed a case where the system refuses to answer, because that was the point — trust. They asked about mistakes and recovery. I said human-in-the-loop is not a nice extra, it’s the product. Keep it short, show the risk, show the fallback. That’s what leadership usually cares about.

七、行为面试：失败、反馈与成长 / Behavioral: Failure, Feedback & Growth
46. Tell me about a time you failed and what you learned. / 请讲一次你失败以及从中学到了什么的经历。

The first version of the knowledge Q&A looked fine in my own tests, but some answers were fluent and wrong. That’s a failure, even if the UI was pretty. I had trusted the model too much. I learned I need chunking, metadata, citation, and a “I don’t know” path. We added evaluation and user feedback after that. The lesson was: in a professional domain, a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer. I still like the project, but that first week taught me more than the happy demo.

47. Tell me about a time you made a mistake. / 请讲一次你犯错的经历。

I once shipped a form change without checking an old browser case on the procurement system. A required field didn’t show up for some users, and they submitted incomplete data. I found it from a production complaint. I fixed it, wrote a note in the team chat, and added that case to my checklist. I didn’t hide it. People were annoyed for a day, then it passed. The mistake itself was small. The important part was owning it fast, before finance ran a whole batch on bad data.

48. Describe a time you received constructive feedback. / 请描述一次你收到建设性反馈的经历。

A colleague told me my PRs were too big. He said reviewers couldn’t see the risk. He was right. I was bundling refactor and feature together because it felt efficient. After that I started splitting: one PR for behavior, one for cleanup if needed. Reviews got faster, and I caught my own bugs earlier. It stung a little, because I thought I was being helpful. Now I see it as respect for other people’s time. That was useful feedback, not just “write nicer code.”

49. Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly. / 请讲一次你快速学习新事物的经历。

When I joined Nokia, I had to deliver on AngularJS pretty fast. I hadn’t used it as my main stack. I didn’t try to learn the whole framework. I picked one real ticket, mapped route to controller to API, and copied the local patterns. Then I upgraded AngularJS Material while doing features. Later I had to write Java unit tests for coverage, which was also new. I used the same method: one real task, not a two-week course. Fast learning, for me, is learning on the path of delivery.

50. Tell me about a time you had to adapt to change. / 请讲一次你适应变化的经历。

My career is basically adapting to change. I started in Python and Django, then the company needed frontend, so I learned React and Taro. Then at Nokia the stack was React plus a legacy AngularJS system, and then AI agents became part of the work. Each time the “main skill” shifted. I don’t love chaos, but I can live with it if the goal is clear. I adapt by keeping one stable habit: understand the business first, then pick the tool. The tool can change. The user problem doesn’t.

51. Describe a time you had to step outside your comfort zone. / 请描述一次你走出舒适区的经历。

When I moved from Django pages to React, I had to unlearn “put a lot of logic next to the template.” Later, with AI, I had to unlearn “the model will just figure it out.” That’s a big one. 54. How do you handle criticism? / 你如何面对批评？

If it’s specific, I take it. If it’s about a bug I shipped, I fix it first, then I think about the process. I don’t like public criticism that is vague, but I still try to ask what they need from me. I remind myself: criticism is cheaper than a production incident. My job is not to protect my ego in the meeting. It’s to leave the meeting with one thing I will do differently. That sounds simple. That users don’t care how clever the code is. They care if the number is right and if they can finish the job. Early on I wanted to show nice components. 八、行为面试：压力、时间管理与优先级 / Behavioral: Pressure, Time Management & Prioritization
56. Tell me about a time you worked under pressure. / 请讲一次你在压力下工作的经历。

Go-live weeks on the bidding platform were stressful. Business wanted the new editor, migration, and listing features together. I broke it down: what must work on day one, what can be ugly but correct, what can wait. I stayed late for the migration script, because bad data would hurt more than a slow page. We shipped the core path. After that I slept. Under pressure I get quieter and more list-based. Panic doesn’t help. A written order of work does.

57. Tell me about a time you had to meet a tight deadline. / 请讲一次你赶截止日期的经历。

For a cutover-related release, we needed real-time progress on the page, and the date was not moving. I cut the nice-to-have UI and focused on WebSocket stability and a clear status. I checked with QA every afternoon, not only at the end. We made the deadline with a simpler page, but it was trustworthy. I’d rather ship a thin, solid thing than a beautiful thing that’s flaky. Tight deadlines force that choice, and I think that’s a healthy choice.

58. How do you prioritize when everything is urgent? / 当所有事情都很紧急时，你如何排优先级？

I don’t accept that everything is equally urgent. I ask three questions: what blocks production, what blocks other people, and what is only loud. Then I tell people the order out loud, so nobody assumes they’re first. If two things are truly urgent, I ask which one can be a smaller version. I also write it down in chat, because memory is bad when everyone is stressed. Priority is a communication tool, not just a personal to-do list.

59. Tell me about a time you had to prioritize multiple tasks. / 请讲一次你同时处理多项任务的经历。

At Nokia I had React features, AngularJS tickets, a component-library upgrade, and the agent work. I couldn’t do them in parallel in my head. I blocked mornings for production-facing bugs, afternoons for feature work, and some evenings for the agent when it was still exploratory. If a production issue came in, it jumped the queue. I told my lead that order, so he could push back if needed. Multi-tasking is usually fake. Sequencing is real.

60. Describe a time you had to make a trade-off. / 请描述一次你不得不权衡取舍的经历。

On the materials platform, we could either make the contract form very complete, or ship on time with the main fields and validation. I chose the main path plus the dangerous fields — amounts, dates, attachments — and left some display-only stuff for later. Business wanted everything. I showed them what would be untested if we took all of it. We shipped, then iterated. Trade-off means someone is a bit unhappy. If nobody is unhappy, you probably didn’t make a real choice.

61. Tell me about a time you had too much on your plate. / 请讲一次你任务过多、应接不暇的经历。

There was a month when I had production bugs, a Material upgrade, and the agent demo in the same window. I was starting to do everything at 70 percent, which is worse than doing fewer things at 100. I went to my lead and said, “If the demo date is real, I need to park the upgrade for a week.” He agreed. That was hard for me, because I like to say yes. Too much on the plate is not a badge. It’s how quality dies quietly.

62. How do you handle stress and pressure? / 你如何应对压力和紧张？

I write the work down and I take a walk for ten minutes if my head is noisy. Then I come back and do the next smallest useful thing. I also talk to teammates early, because stress gets worse when I hide. On weekends I play pool with two or three friends. It sounds small, but it resets me. I don’t pretend I’m never stressed. I just try not to let stress decide the quality of the release.

63. Tell me about a time you had to work overtime or go the extra mile. / 请讲一次你加班或额外付出努力的经历。

I did overtime before the procurement modules went live, and also when I was building the agent. The agent one was more extra mile — evenings, reading docs, trying frameworks, then building the UI and the RAG pipeline. I don’t think overtime should be the culture. But when a go-live is real, or when I’m learning something the team needs, I will stay. Then I recover. Extra mile is okay as an exception. If it’s every week, the plan is wrong.

64. How do you stay organized? / 你如何保持工作有条理？

I’m not a super fancy system person. I keep a daily list: must do, can wait, waiting on others. I use tickets, and I write short notes after meetings, especially field names and rules. In Git I try to keep PRs small. For bigger work, I make a simple checklist, like I did for the UI upgrade. Organization for me is just making sure I don’t keep the project only in my head. If I got hit by a bus, someone should still understand the next step.

65. Tell me about a time you had to say no to a request because of priorities. / 请讲一次你因为优先级而拒绝请求的经历。

During a settlement release, someone asked for a nicer export format. It was reasonable, but it wasn’t the priority. Finance needed the numbers to be right. I said, “Not this week. I’ll put it in the next sprint.” I also explained why: if I context-switch now, the risky part gets less testing. They accepted it because I didn’t disappear. I gave a time. Saying no without a when is what makes people angry.

九、情景假设类 / Situational Questions
66. How would you handle multiple urgent tasks at the same time? / 如果同时有多个紧急任务，你会怎么处理？

I’d first check which one is actually production-breaking. That one goes first. Then I’d see if any task is blocking other people, because that’s expensive. I’d tell everyone the order in one message, so they don’t all think they’re number one. If I can’t do them all, I’d ask which one can be a smaller version, or who else can take a slice. I wouldn’t silently hero-work all night and then miss both. Urgent work still needs a queue.

67. What would you do if you disagreed with your manager's decision? / 如果你不同意经理的决定，你会怎么做？

I’d disagree once, with reasons, in private if possible. I’d say the risk, the user impact, and an alternative. If they still decide, I’ll execute, unless it’s unethical or unsafe. I’m not going to fight the same point every day. Managers see timeline and politics I don’t always see. My job is to make sure they decide with the real technical risk on the table. After that, I align. A team can’t have two directions.

68. How would you deal with a team member who is not pulling their weight? / 如果团队中有人不出力，你会怎么处理？

I’d talk to them first, privately, and ask what’s going on. Maybe they’re stuck, or the task is unclear. I’d help them split the work into smaller pieces. If nothing changes, I’d escalate with facts: this ticket, this delay, this impact. I wouldn’t gossip, and I wouldn’t cover for them forever, because that trains the team that one person can hide. Be kind, but don’t be naive.

69. What would you do if you made a mistake that affected the team? / 如果你犯了影响团队的错误，你会怎么做？

I’d say it immediately, in the group that needs to know. What happened, what’s the impact, what I’m doing now, what I need from others. Then I’d fix it, write a short note so we don’t repeat it, and not disappear from shame. After that I’d check if anyone else is blocked because of me. Owning it fast usually costs one uncomfortable hour. Hiding it can cost a whole release.

70. How would you handle a difficult customer or client? / 如果遇到难缠的客户，你会怎么处理？

I’d listen first and let them finish. Difficult often means they’re scared or blocked. Then I’d repeat the problem in my words, so they know I got it. After that I separate: what we can do now, what we can’t, and when. If they’re changing requirements every day, I’d write them down and ask them to rank. Stay calm, stay specific. I wouldn’t match their emotion. I also wouldn’t promise a date I can’t keep just to make the meeting nicer.

71. What would you do if your manager asked you to do something unethical? / 如果经理让你做不道德的事，你会怎么做？

I would say no. I’m pretty clear on that. I wouldn’t fake data, hide a safety issue, or cheat an audit, even if it’s “just this once.” I’d explain why I can’t, and suggest a legal way if there is one. If they insist, I’d take it up through the proper channel. Especially in a company like Volkswagen, with cars and production, trust is the business. I want this job, but not at that price.

72. How would you handle a project with unclear requirements? / 如果项目需求不清晰，你会怎么处理？

I wouldn’t wait for a perfect document. I’d write down what I think the user is trying to do, make a thin demo or a flow, and ask the business to correct me. I’d also list the open questions: data source, edge cases, who can approve. Then we freeze a smallest version. Unclear requirements are normal. Building for two weeks in silence is the real mistake. I learned that on procurement forms — the form looks clear until you ask about one special coal type.

73. What would you do if you had to deliver a project late? / 如果项目必须延期交付，你会怎么做？

I’d say it as soon as I know, with a new date I actually believe. I’d show what’s done, what’s not, and what we can cut to still give value. Maybe we ship the main flow and delay the extra reports. I’d also say why — not “too busy,” but “this migration is riskier than we thought.” Then I’d over-communicate until it’s out. Late is bad. Silent and late is worse.

74. How would you handle a sudden change in priorities? / 如果优先级突然变化，你会怎么应对？

I’d pause, write down what I’m currently holding, and ask: what do we stop, what do we pause, what still has to finish today? Then I’d park the old work in a state someone else can pick up — notes, branch, ticket. I wouldn’t try to keep both in my head. Sudden change is fine. Half-switching two projects is how you create bugs. I’d also confirm the new deadline in writing, just so we’re not playing telephone.

75. What would you do if you had to work with someone you don’t get along with? / 如果必须和合不来的人共事，你会怎么做？

I don’t need to be friends with everyone. I need a working agreement. I’d keep communication on the work: tickets, APIs, dates. I’d be polite, a bit more written, a bit less small talk if that’s what keeps it calm. If the relationship is really affecting delivery, I’d still talk once, fact-based. I wouldn’t let personal dislike become a project risk. At the end of the day, we both want the thing to ship. That’s enough common ground to start.
